Scam Ray

A system I designed that makes and posts a short scam-awareness video every day on YouTube, TikTok and Instagram. Claude wrote most of the code. I designed how it works, made the product and architecture calls, and approve every video before it posts.

What it does

From a scam tip to a posted video

Each step starts the next one on its own. My hands-on steps are approving each video and setting its YouTube cover.

1On each tip email

Research

Topics come from FTC, FBI, USPS and DOJ scam alerts and from viewer tips. When a tip email arrives, the app files it, removes the viewer's personal details and starts a Claude research job. The researched tip shows up in the app with its sources, and I decide whether it becomes a video.

2When the queue runs low

Scripts

After each post, the app counts the approved videos still waiting. When only a few are left, it starts Claude writing a new batch. Every claim is checked online with sources cited, and each script follows the house style, the safety rules and any test that's running.

3When a script is saved

Video

GitHub Actions turns each script into a video in code: a neural voice, word-by-word captions, animated phone, laptop and tablet screens, real photos with the background cut out, and sound. First, a check confirms the phone numbers, card numbers and web addresses can't belong to anyone real.

4When a video is ready

My review

The video and its covers land on my review screen, with a phone alert, within a minute. I approve it or leave a note. Nothing posts without my OK.

5When I leave a note

Fixes

The AI sorts each note first. Script fixes are made inside the app in seconds for a few cents. Changes that need new code go to a full Claude coding session. Both send the fixed video back to my review screen.

69:00am daily

Posting

The next approved video posts to YouTube, TikTok and Instagram, with safe retries that never double-post. The public website adds it, the app reminds me to set the YouTube cover, and daily stats from all three apps are collected for my A/B tests.

Running in the background

A nightly backup of the database to Google Drive, an alert whenever a scheduled job misses its time, single-user sign-in, and a hard monthly cap on AI spending.

Key decisions I made

My first call, then each step in the order a video moves through the system.

  • Full automation. I turned down Claude's first plan, which had me editing every video by hand, and asked for a fully automated pipeline.
  • Event-driven tips. Viewer tips used to wait for a scheduled AI check of the inbox. I redesigned it so the moment a tip email arrives, the app files it, removes the viewer's personal details and starts the AI research itself. The researched tip shows up in the app within minutes, and the AI never needs access to the inbox.
  • A queue, not a calendar. Videos were first planned by day, Day 1 to Day 7, which stopped making sense once posting was automatic. I changed it to a queue: when only a few approved videos are left, the app starts the AI writing a new batch, following the rules in my Settings.
  • Rendered in code. I chose to render every video in code instead of paying for AI-generated video, which would have cost about $300 a month. The project is meant to show automation, and code gives the same reliable look every day.
  • Two paths for fixes. A note on one video can need two different kinds of AI. A script fix runs inside the app on pay-per-use AI in seconds, for a few cents. A change that needs new code, like a new kind of screen, goes to a full Claude coding session. I designed the hand-off: the AI sorts each note first and shows me where it will go, and both paths send the finished video back to the same review screen for my approval.
  • Posting on three apps. Each app had its own rules for automatic posting. I filed YouTube's API review myself and ran an unlisted test upload to confirm our videos wouldn't get locked. TikTok only allowed drafts and Instagram blocked my developer sign-up, so I posted by hand in the meantime, then set up a posting service both apps already approved and tested a public post before switching it on. Since October 14, every daily post has gone out to all three apps on its own.
  • An overnight move. I set 14 requirements for the new app, like one-person sign-in and no double posts, and we moved it overnight so no daily post was missed.
  • Least-privileged security. I built security around least-privileged access for each part. Keys are stored as secrets, never in code or chat, and I added each one myself. The GitHub and Claude keys I created are limited to what they need and expire on a schedule. Email access is limited to the channel's own labels, viewers who send tips are never named, steps that touch private data wait for my typed OK, and I kept AI browser tools away from my signed-in accounts.
  • One private app. The public website first ran from a code folder anyone could read. I moved it into the private admin app, so one Next.js app now runs both and all the code stays private.

What I designed

The admin app

Early on, the review page didn't say what to do next, and changing a post time meant asking the AI in chat. I designed an admin app with a Home page that lists what needs me, upcoming posts in the order they'll go out, a Post now button and a Settings page for post times and batch rules.

Swipe-to-review

Reviewing videos one at a time on a phone was slow. I designed a review screen that works like a dating app: swipe between videos, approve with a tap or leave a note, and approve the video's covers at the same time. Every video still goes through it.

Desktop and phone layouts

The first layout was cramped and changed from page to page. I led the redesign one change at a time, choosing from mockups more than 30 times: a side menu on desktop, a bottom menu on phones, sticky headers and a video page with the queue beside the player.

The brand and video look

The first videos looked cheap and the colors felt scary. I set the brand: a white logo with SCAM in black and RAY in red, our own background instead of stock footage, and sticker colors that mean something, yellow for caution and red for stop. I also asked for scenes that show how people reach a scam, like clicking an ad or scanning a QR code.

The hard part

Problems I solved

Platforms

Two apps said no to automatic posting

Instagram blocked my developer sign-up, and TikTok said it would only take drafts. I ran a real public test to confirm the limit, then found a posting service both apps already approved and tested it before switching it on. All three apps have posted on their own since October 14, 30 of 30.

Monitoring

A usage warning that hid a silent failure

My hosting plan warned I was near its free limit. The screenshots I gathered led to the real cause: after a web address change, new-email alerts had failed about 13,000 times in six hours, so viewer tips had quietly stopped. It was fixed about three days before the plan would have paused posting.

AI quality

AI fixes that didn't fix anything

My review notes went to a quick AI editor even when the change needed new code, and once it “fixed” a note without changing the video. I chose to show where each note will go before it's sent, caught wrong labels in a live test, and approved a stronger model at about 2¢ a note.

Safety

Fake details that could be real

I asked whether a phone number in a video was real. It had a real area code, so I had the video remade. I asked the same about a card number and a made-up street that turned out to exist. Each question became an automatic check every script must pass.

Data

Early numbers that made videos worse

The AI used five days of stats to change a whole batch, and it came out choppy. I set the rule that numbers never change the house style on their own. Instead, two versions run across upcoming videos as an A/B test, and a test starts only when I approve it.

Design

Alerts I couldn't act on

Notices showed up twice, opened the wrong page and didn't clear. I mapped every notice, alert and link in the app and redesigned them one section at a time. Problems now go to an alert bell, and everyday tasks go to a Do next list. Each alert names the video by its title and opens the exact page I need, and each kind of phone alert has its own on/off switch. I'm still working through the last sections.

Setup

Logins that would expire every week

Google's test mode makes logins expire every seven days, which would have broken automatic posting. I published the app to production and proved to Google that we own the website. I also set up the Google Cloud project, the YouTube connection and the TikTok developer app myself.

Cost

Running out of free build minutes

Video builds used up the free minutes and stopped every job, which would have stopped the next morning's post. Instead of buying a subscription, I set a $5 spending limit, and builds now redo only the videos that changed.

Kangaroo

An AI assistant I'm building that helps you make a sound call on every meeting invite. Kangaroo reads each new invite, then suggests what to do, with short reasons: accept, catch up later, ask for details, or skip with a polite note drafted from your own files. The problem

Too many meetings, and too little to go on

Knowledge workers get more meeting invites than they can usefully attend, and many arrive with little to go on: no agenda, no clear purpose, sometimes no location. Without that, it's hard to tell whether they're needed, so most people accept by default. They lose hours to meetings where they only listen, and walk into others unsure why they're there or what to bring.

Many of those meetings could be a short written update, but declining is awkward. They have to judge which ones are safe to skip, write a polite reply, and make sure the organizer gets what they need from them.

Kangaroo is built first for individual professionals on Microsoft 365 who use Outlook and OneDrive. Sign-in works with Microsoft, Google or Apple. Power users, organizations and people who want to self-host the open-source core come in later phases.

How it works

Six steps, with you in control

1Within 1 min

Watch

Microsoft Graph notifications report new and changed invites, and a daily sync catches anything missed. Each invite is handled once.

2Per invite

Check and suggest

Kangaroo looks for what you'd need to decide, like an agenda, a purpose and a location, then suggests one of four responses: accept, catch up later, ask the organizer for details, or skip with a polite note. Each comes with up to three short reasons.

3Where you look

Show

The suggestion and its buttons arrive in an alert email, the nightly Day in Review and the portal. A version that puts them inside the invite email itself works in testing.

4On request

Draft

Skip notes are drafted from files in a OneDrive folder you link. The files are read in memory and never stored.

5One tap

Approve

You tap your choice. Links open a short confirm page, so a mail scanner opening links can never act for you.

6On approval

Act

Kangaroo checks that the invite hasn't changed since you approved it, then acts once through Graph and logs it.

The goal

Automatic triage

Today, Kangaroo suggests and the user taps. The goal is for it to handle invites on its own when they pass a threshold each user sets. It's planned for the pilot phase and isn't built yet.

Opt-in

Off until turned on

Automatic triage stays off unless the user turns it on. The portal shows it as "Coming soon" until it's ready.

Per calendar

Each user's own threshold

Each connected calendar gets its own setting and threshold, so a work calendar and a personal one can follow different rules.

Reviewed nightly

Easy to check and undo

Anything Kangaroo handles on its own is planned to show up in Day in Review, with a one-tap undo.

Key decisions I made

How Kangaroo is built, how its data is organized, and how it connects to Microsoft 365.

  • Data layer. I moved the backend from Microsoft Dataverse to PostgreSQL with Prisma, and upgraded sign-in to Auth.js v5.
  • Many accounts, one user. One Kangaroo user can link many sign-ins, calendars and file sources from different providers, because real people have work and personal accounts and keep files in more than one place.
  • One provider list. A single list drives sign-up, sign-in and every Connect button, so adding a provider means changing one place.
  • Revocable sessions. Every page load checks for an active user, so access can be cut off at once.
  • Settings on each calendar. Auto-decline, alerts and Day in Review settings live on each connected calendar instead of one account page, because different calendars need different rules. I built the account-wide page first and replaced it.
  • Server-side, not an add-in. The first plan put the Outlook add-in first. I'd already moved away from it, so Kangaroo runs on the server and works without Outlook open.
  • Swappable AI layer. Any AI provider connects through one adapter, to avoid lock-in and let self-hosters bring their own model.
  • Open core as a clean copy. Open source with bring-your-own model, developed privately, then published as a clean copy with no history after launch.
  • Four choices, not two. I added "Catch up later," which accepts quietly and shows the time as free, because declining an all-hands looks bad and Tentative reads as doubt. I also added "Ask for details," because many invites have no agenda and you can't make a sound call without one, and had it built now and sent directly instead of later as a draft.
  • Decide where the invite already is. Kangaroo swaps the invite email for its own version with the suggestion and working buttons, so there's no extra email or trip to the portal. It works today as an admin test.
  • Least-access follow-ups. When an organizer answers a details request, Kangaroo reads only that one email thread, never the rest of the inbox.
  • Its own address and monitoring. Kangaroo sends from its own address, with replies going to a monitored support inbox, and keeps its own error log and Health page instead of a third-party tracker.
  • Nothing unfinished in view. Every page users can reach is finished, hidden or marked "Coming soon."

The hardest problems in the build, and how I worked through them.

Planning

Plans that ignored what was built

The first plan put the add-in first, which I'd already moved away from, and a later plan for the settings step ignored my per-calendar pages. I caught both. Kangaroo became server-side, and the settings step was rebuilt on an audit of my pages.

Suggestions

Labels that contradicted their reasons

One invite's reasons said skip while its label said keep, and later every test invite came back "keep" without a real evaluation. I caught both. Each led to an engine fix and a fresh re-score.

Writing

Notes that didn't sound human

Details requests asked a lunch for an agenda, ignored a missing location, used odd formats and always signed off the same way. I flagged each one, and after five fixes the notes read like normal email.

Outlook

Buttons that vanished

Testing in Outlook on the web, Mac and phone, I found the buttons only showed on the web, then disappeared after a redesign. The card was rebuilt for the older format Mac and phones draw, and a line of text was added because Outlook hides a card with none.

Testing

A test that couldn't fail

The test invites had only two people, no matching files, and stated their own answers. I had realistic guest lists and a file-citation check added to Gate 1, and proposed harder tiered tests before pilots.

Testing

My test account gets almost no real invites, so I had a test calendar send scripted ones. I also caught that the test had 27 invites instead of 30, and that three were set for a Saturday when the sender only runs on weekdays.

What it does

A rep's day, from first check-in to Friday report

Salespeople often cover dozens of organizations, with history spread across email, chat, the CRM and spreadsheets. Prospecting Desk pulls it into one to-do list that knows the history with every person.

1Each morning

Check-in

The app writes a prompt, the rep's AI assistant searches their email and calendar, and one pasted table brings back replies, booked meetings, out-of-office notes and "not now" answers. A review screen shows every change before it's saved.

2Today

Who to contact next

Everyone eligible is ranked, from referrals and upcoming renewals to accounts that have gone quiet. Each person needs a real reason to reach out, like a renewal or a past conversation, never a generic "checking in."

3Outreach

Drafts for the whole day

Timed email, call and LinkedIn sequences, with every draft and call script for the day written by AI in one paste, using each person's history. The rep edits and sends. The app never sends anything itself.

4Meetings

Prep and follow-up

A prep brief before each meeting, then a recap email and a dated next step after. A no-show starts a short rebooking sequence.

5Deals

Nothing slips

Quiet quotes and renewals move to Hot Follow-ups with a revival draft. "Check back in the spring" is saved with the date and comes back on its own, with an opener that quotes what they said.

6Fridays

Report and learn

The week's meetings and activity, counted in the team's own categories. What's working compares reply and meeting rates and suggests changes the rep can apply or undo.

Built for each rep

The kit is two files: a core app that ships empty, and a setup prompt. A rep attaches both to a Claude chat, and Claude interviews them one tap-to-answer question at a time about how they sell, like the kinds of buyers they reach and how their territory is organized. It never asks for real accounts, customers or deals, and it stops anyone who starts pasting them. Claude shows a one-screen plan for approval, edits the app's code to fit, and tests the build in a browser before handing it over. Real data is loaded into the finished app afterward, on the rep's own computer.

Three levels of automation

Semi-automated works on day one with copy and paste. The recommended start is semi-automated with automation built in and switched off until IT approves. Fully automated has a scheduled flow drop results into a synced folder for the app to pick up. Every level goes through the same review screens.

Key decisions I made

How the app is built, how data moves through it, and how it's shared.

  • Architecture. I chose a single HTML file that runs in the browser, with no server, install or account, and with the rep's own AI assistant doing the thinking behind it.
  • AI as the data layer. Instead of templates and rules alone, the app has the AI assistant gather and build the information it runs on, then the app plans the day from it.
  • One-paste data flow. Each AI result comes back as one bulk paste instead of a form full of boxes, through a single Paste button. A marker in the AI's answer tells the app which batch just arrived.
  • Data model. I had accounts modeled as parent organizations with their sites and central offices, and focus areas grouped under solution areas, each type with its own rule.
  • A rules layer for plays. I designed Strategies so a rep's plays control who gets contacted, how often and with what angle. Strategy notes are context for the AI, which turns them into talking points instead of copying them.
  • Single user by design. Each app is private to one person, and the sync file is a personal backup, not a shared database.
  • The shared kit's design. A tested foundation app plus a setup prompt. Every build starts from a cleaned copy of the full app instead of a description, which kept the design and quality the same across builds.
  • Logic separate from content. The kit ships the engine only. Templates, scripts and playbooks are written fresh for each rep, so no one's approach is built in.
  • A prompt audit. I asked for a review of every AI prompt in the app and the data each one uses.

The hard part

Problems I caught and solved

The hardest design problems in the build, and how I worked through them.

Personalization

Making one app fit every rep

The first approach built each rep's app from a written description, and it came out far plainer than the original. I chose a different base: every build starts from a cleaned copy of the full, tested app, and Claude customizes its code for each rep. Every build now keeps the same design and engine.

Separation

Keeping personal data out of shared code

When I reviewed the shared copy, I found leftover personal settings, report categories, courtesy numbers and outreach playbooks still inside it. That led to a clean split: the kit ships the engine only, and everything personal is written fresh for each rep during setup.

AI reliability

AI that invents data

An AI assistant admitted it had made up contacts it returned. I had every AI-added contact deleted and replaced with one full sweep, and the app now requires a source for each found contact and sends every paste through a review screen.

Data quality

Messy AI output

Pasted results came back with brackets, links and citation tags, and some dates were a day off. I reported each case with the exact pattern so the parsing could be fixed, and designed a marker in the AI's answer so the app knows which batch arrived.

Data integrity

Counts that didn't add up

I caught meetings importing twice, group meetings counted once per person, and activity counted that never happened. The fixes made each meeting a single record however many people attend, and the weekly numbers count only work logged in the app.

Testing

Testing a prompt, not just code

A setup prompt can't be unit-tested like code. Claude ran five builds as made-up reps from start to finish. I approved each run, reviewed what it found, and chose to fix every gap before sharing the kit.

Workflow Automation

Four Power Automate flows I designed and built by hand for a lead-to-consultation process. They nurture each lead with emails written for its segment, write every Calendly booking back to the right lead, honor unsubscribes right away, and keep an expense sheet up to date, all with Cognito Forms as the lead database.

The goal

From new lead to booked consultation, without manual follow-up

The process had two streams of leads, one from an intake form and one from the public, and each lead was tracked and followed up by hand. The goal was for every lead to get the right emails, every booking recorded on the lead, and every unsubscribe honored at once.

  • Easy to maintain. Simple to keep running, with an intuitive setup.
  • Easy to grow. New campaigns, lead types and meeting types can be added without a rebuild.
  • No manual tracking. Leads move through the process on their own.
  • Existing tools. Built on a Microsoft 365 Business Standard plan, with Cognito Forms staying the lead database.
The flows

Four flows, one process

Each flow runs on its own, and they share the same lead record, so a booking or unsubscribe in one changes what the others do.

1New lead

Segmented lead nurture

Each new lead is sorted into one of two categories, one with six subtypes split by time frame, and gets up to four timed emails written for its segment. Before each email, the flow re-checks the lead's record and skips the send if they've booked a consultation or unsubscribed. If a consultation is cancelled and not rebooked, the emails pick back up.

2Calendly webhook

Consultation scheduling

The intake form and every email send prospects to Calendly with the lead's two IDs attached. When a meeting is booked, a webhook starts the flow, which matches both IDs to the lead and writes the date and time to their record. A cancellation clears it, waits to give the prospect time to rebook, then sends a reschedule email.

3Unsubscribe form

Unsubscribe compliance

Every email carries an unsubscribe link with the lead's IDs. It opens a form that confirms the prospect's email address and shows whether they're already unsubscribed. One button sets the lead to inactive, which stops every email, in line with CAN-SPAM.

4Daily schedule

Expense tracking

A daily flow logs monthly and annual recurring expenses into an Excel table, with formulas for the running balance, a totals row and filters by month, year and type. Only revenue and one-off expenses are entered by hand.

Key decisions I made

How the data is set up, how the flows find the right lead, and how the system grows.

  • Clean data first. Before automating anything, I made every needed field required on the lead form and added hidden fields for marketing status and an auto-generated case number, so every flow has complete data to work with.
  • Two IDs on every lead. Each lead carries an entry ID and a case number, and the flows match on both, so a booking or an unsubscribe can't update the wrong person.
  • IDs carried through links. Both IDs travel as URL parameters into Calendly and the unsubscribe form, so the flows know exactly which lead acted without asking anyone to type anything.
  • Check before every send. Instead of a fixed sequence, the nurture flow re-reads the lead's record after each wait, so it stops the moment someone books or unsubscribes.
  • The case number picks the path. The case number shows whether a lead came from intake or the public, so the scheduling flow knows which workflow to follow.
  • Built to grow. Email frameworks are in place for future lead subtypes, and the scheduling flow is set up for more meeting types, so adding one means filling in a framework instead of rebuilding.
  • Existing tools only. Everything runs on a Microsoft 365 Business Standard plan, plus the forms and scheduling tools already in place.
The hard part

Problems I solved

Integration

Knowing who booked

Calendly doesn't know which lead is booking. I passed both IDs as UTM parameters, so Calendly saves them with each booking and sends them through its webhook to Power Automate.

Error handling

Bookings that don't match

If the IDs don't match a lead, the flow posts the flow name, a link to the run, the scheduler, the time and both IDs to a Teams channel, so someone can fix the record right away.

Error handling

Updates that fail

If a cancelled booking can't be cleared from the lead, the flow pauses and sends an approval request with the details. Once they fix the entry and approve, the flow carries on.

Logic

Cancellations

A cancelled consultation clears the lead's booking, waits to give them time to rebook, then sends a reschedule email. If they don't rebook, their nurture emails resume.

Security

A safe unsubscribe link

The form only finds a lead when both IDs match, and it shows the email address on file before anything changes, so a guessed link can't unsubscribe someone else.

Email

Designed emails from Outlook

I converted the email templates to HTML so the Outlook connector could send them as designed, with a button to book a consultation and a link to unsubscribe.

What I'm interested in · Research notes

Connected data, built around each person

I'm interested in software that brings a company's data together, shapes itself around each person who uses it, and gives them an AI assistant that works across all of it. Prospecting Desk is where I'm starting.

1 · Observation

Three things slow people down

1Scattered

Information is scattered

A single job can involve email, a calendar, files, chat and several other systems. Seeing what other teams are doing adds even more places to check.

~1,200app switches a day in a study of 137 workers. Refocusing cost about 9% of their work time.[1]
2One size

Tools are built for the average person

Software is designed once for everyone in a role. But people in the same job plan, read and decide differently, so one design fits few of them well.

This is the part I'm testing with Prospecting Desk. See the hypothesis below.
3Partial

AI only sees part of the picture

The AI built into each tool only knows that tool's data. It can help inside one system, but it can't organize someone's whole day or connect what's happening across teams.

2 · Hypothesis

One connected view, a different app for each person

A connected layer brings every team's records together, with the access each person already has. On top of it, each person gets an app built around their role and the way they work, with its own views, wording and rules. AI coding tools make that practical, because a personal build can come out of one guided conversation instead of a months-long project. And because the data is connected, an AI assistant can work across all of it to organize each person's day and help with the work itself.

My hypothesis

If a person's information is connected in one place, their app is built around how they work, and an AI assistant can see everything they're allowed to see, they'll spend less time hunting for information and more time on the work itself.

A made-up example

A tenant calls a property manager about a leak. Today, the manager checks the repair request in the maintenance system, the lease in the leasing system and the rent history in accounting. With one connected view, all three are on the same screen, laid out the way that manager likes to see things, and AI has already drafted a reply to the tenant with the repair date.

A supporting view

Ink & Switch's essay on malleable software argues that people should be able to reshape the software they use, and that AI code generation helps but doesn't remove every barrier on its own.[2] That's why I pair AI with a tested core and a guided setup, instead of asking people to build their own tools.

1Connected

One connected view

Every team keeps its own system, and the connected layer brings their records together. Nobody keeps a private copy of the truth.

2Personal

An app for each person

The views, wording, rules and order of the day fit the person using it, so the app works the way they already think about their job.

3Assisted

AI that works across it all

With every team's records connected, AI can organize your day, flag what needs you and draft the next step. It sees only what you're already allowed to see.

4Consistent

Guardrails stay the same

Privacy rules, review steps and the tested core stay the same in every build, so personal never means unsafe or unreliable.

3 · Proposed design

From scattered systems to your own app

1Step 1

Connect

Link the systems each team already uses. No one has to switch tools, and access follows the permissions people already have.

2Step 2

Learn how you work

A short setup interview asks how you work and take in information, like "Do you plan your day from a list, a calendar or a map?"

3Step 3

Build your app

Your app is built from a tested core, with your own views, wording, rules and level of detail.

4Step 4

Stay in step with other teams

Start by seeing other teams' updates that affect your work, with AI organizing them for you. Later, reply to, update or assign work in their systems from your own app.

4 · The test

Testing the personal app with Prospecting Desk

Prospecting Desk tests the personal-app half on one role. One tested core app is shared by everyone, and a setup interview turns it into a version built for each salesperson.

  • A single core app holds every view and engine, with no one's wording or numbers in it.
  • A setup interview of about 30 to 55 questions captures how each person sells: their buyers, goals, tone and courtesy rules.
  • Claude customizes the core app's code for each person, so every build keeps the same design and tested engine.
  • Each person's data stays with them, and every AI result is reviewed before it's saved.
What I'm exploring next

Shaping how the app shows things, based on how each person takes in information.

For someone who thinks in pictures

Accounts on a map or a color-coded board instead of a table, with coverage gaps visible at a glance.

For someone who likes one thing at a time

Today shows a single next step, and the rest of the list stays tucked away until it's done.

For someone who wants the big picture first

The day opens with goal pace and the week's plan, with the details one tap away.

For someone who wants every detail

Full history, dates and numbers up front, with nothing hidden behind a click.

For someone who learns by listening

Meeting prep and the morning summary read aloud, for a rep who prepares on the drive.

For someone who works in bursts

Calls, emails and research grouped into focused blocks instead of one mixed list.
