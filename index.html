Scam Ray

An automated content pipeline for a consumer education channel about scams. Each day it researches a current scam, writes a fact-checked script, produces a short video and publishes it to YouTube, TikTok and Instagram at 9:00am. The only manual step is approving each video. Daily analytics and an A/B testing Ideas Center feed what works back into the scripts.

100%on-time scheduled posts, Oct 11–15

The goal

A daily channel on a $0 tool budget

Build a daily video channel that operates within a $0 tool budget and 1 to 3 hours of weekly oversight. I chose full automation over manual video editing, since editing each video by hand would not scale.

During the first week, while platform API approvals were pending, I published every video manually on all three platforms. That took about 30 minutes a day and defined the steps the system needed to replace.

How it works

Six steps, one approval

From scam alert to published video, every step runs on its own except human review.

1Continuous

Research

Claude routines monitor FTC, FBI and USPS alerts and viewer submissions, and remove personal information.

28:55am daily

Scripting

When the approved queue falls below four videos, Claude drafts seven fact-checked scripts with cited sources.

3On commit

Production

GitHub Actions generates narration, visuals and captions, and validates that sample web addresses are unregistered.

4Within 1 min

Human review

A signed webhook delivers each video to a private review app with a mobile notification. I approve it or submit revision notes.

5As needed

Revisions

Revision notes go to the Claude API, which rewrites the script for re-rendering at about $0.03 per revision.

69:00am daily

Publishing

A scheduled database job publishes to all three platforms and updates the public website.

The hard part

Getting platform approval

The main obstacles were platform access policies, not code. Each platform restricted automated publishing differently, so I rolled out automation one platform at a time.

Architecture

Moving to a standalone app

The first version ran in a Claude.ai artifact coordinated by six scheduled routines. Actions waited for the next scheduled run and storage was limited, so I migrated it to a dedicated web app and database.

How I ran the migration

I defined 14 requirements up front, including single-user authentication, row-level security on every table, no client-side secrets, verified webhooks and duplicate-post prevention. I required a system summary and an approved plan before development began, then reviewed each of seven stages before the next started. The migration was completed in one day.

Instant approvals

Approval actions take effect immediately instead of waiting hours.

Faster submissions

Viewer submissions arrive in under a minute instead of twice daily.

Less waste

About 720 unused GitHub Actions minutes per month were eliminated.

Analytics

Three apps' stats, collected automatically

Each app keeps its numbers in its own dashboard, in its own terms, and checking three apps by hand every day didn't scale. A daily job now collects every Short's stats from YouTube, TikTok and Instagram into one page in my review app, and those stats feed the Ideas Center.

Daily sync

A GitHub Actions job pulls per-Short and channel stats from the YouTube Analytics and Data APIs, and TikTok and Instagram stats from the publishing partner's analytics API.

Plain math for the numbers

The app works out shares, averages and per-app comparisons itself. No AI touches the numbers.

A weekly "What's different"

A Sunday Claude routine writes 3 to 5 points on how viewers differ on each app, each ending with what to try, and says so plainly when an app has too few views to tell.

Counts only

Only counts and percentages are saved. No comments, captions or viewer details are stored.

Ideas Center

Turning numbers into tested rules

YouTube's built-in "Test & compare" doesn't work for Shorts. The Ideas Center gives every idea a fair test, a clear verdict and a paper trail, from idea to test to result to rule.

1Weekly or anytime

Idea

Ideas come from the Sunday routine, with a "why" backed by numbers, or from me. The AI may suggest one only when 3 or more Shorts agree, and each idea changes one thing.

2Across Shorts

Test

Upcoming Shorts alternate between version A and version B. The AI script writer writes and tags each one to match, and the tag shows on my review card.

3Plain rules

Score

The deciding number is average % watched on YouTube, counted 3 days after posting. A side wins only with a 10-point lead and most of its head-to-head pairs. Anything closer is "Too close to call".

4My call

Adopt

I choose Adopt, Run again or Keep the old way. An adopted rule goes into the Playbook and the script rules every new Short follows.

First test: in progress

"Does a quick scam call-out open better than a story?" I extended it from 7 to 14 Shorts per side. As of October 17, 7 Shorts have posted for A and 4 for B. There's no verdict yet, so the Playbook is still empty.

Problems solved

Tiny numbers, noisy results

With tens of views per Short, I judge on average % watched instead of views, and pair Shorts in posting order so one lucky topic can't carry a side.

Late YouTube numbers

Each Short counts only after a 3-day settle, and the result date shown accounts for it.

Stats without my own developer apps

TikTok and Meta wouldn't approve my own apps, so I used the partner that already posts my videos. A small read-only test proved its analytics were free before I built anything.

Testing caught an AI mistake

Claude first said TikTok doesn't share watch time or traffic sources. The test-first check showed it does, so the plan was corrected before the build.

Video changes vs app-only changes

A change to the video reaches all three apps, so it's always tested. A caption, hashtag or post-time change for one app becomes a rule without a test.

A styling leak

New Ideas Center styles restyled rows on the live home page. Each section's styles are now scoped to it, as a standing rule for the AI.

Analytics and the Ideas Center add $0 a month. They run on the Claude plan and free tiers I already use, and the scoring is plain arithmetic.

A regression shifted captions in two videos. I added full-length caption checks before review.

A generated card number passed checksum validation. I added strict rules for sample data.

Analytics changes

Applying early data to a full batch reduced quality. Changes are now limited to one controlled test at a time.

Open-core SaaS · Private alpha

Kangaroo

An AI assistant that helps you skip meetings you don't need to attend. It watches your Outlook calendar, suggests what to do with each new invite, and puts that suggestion and its buttons right into the invite email. Nothing happens until you tap.

The problem

Too many meetings, and declining is awkward

Knowledge workers are invited to more meetings than they can usefully attend. Many of those invites could be handled with a short written update instead of 30 to 60 minutes of everyone's time. Declining is awkward, though: you have to decide which meetings are safe to skip, write a polite reply, and still give the organizer what they needed from you. So most people accept by default and lose hours to meetings where they only listen.

Kangaroo is built first for individual professionals on Microsoft 365 who use Outlook and OneDrive. Later plans cover power users, organizations, and technical users who want to self-host the open-source core.

Where it stands

The core engine is built

Every core engine step is done and running on a test environment against my own calendar. A two-week test on real invites begins October 19.

Suggestions inside the invite

Kangaroo replaces the invite email with its own version that shows its suggestion, its reasons and working buttons, so you can decide without leaving your inbox.

Notes drafted from your files

When you decline, Kangaroo drafts a short, polite note from files in a linked OneDrive folder, such as a status update, and you can edit it before it sends.

Day in Review

One email a day, at the hour you choose, lists anything you haven't decided yet, with the same one-tap choices.

Real Outlook actions

Declines, quiet accepts and accepts go through Microsoft Graph, so the organizer's calendar updates, and every action is written to an audit log.

Monitoring built in

An error log, throttled email alerts and an admin Health page show job run times and failures, without storing tokens or email content.

The account layer

Sign-in with Microsoft, Google or Apple, linked accounts and calendars, and a protected portal, which I built by hand before this phase.

Earlier prototypes

Before the portal, I built an Outlook add-in that pulled real meetings from selected calendars through Microsoft Graph, in the user's own time zone, with a Decline and Draft Reply flow. I also built a React wireframe for the web app with custom brand illustrations. I parked the add-in when the product moved to a server-side design, which doesn't require users to install anything.

How it works

Six steps, with you approving every action

1Within 1 min

Watch

Microsoft Graph notifications report new and changed invites, and a daily sync catches anything missed. Each invite is handled once.

2Per invite

Triage

Kangaroo's decision engine suggests one of four actions with up to three short reasons.

3In the inbox

Show

The suggestion and its buttons appear in the invite email, in Day in Review and in the portal.

4On request

Draft

Notes are drafted from your linked files, which are read in memory and never stored.

5One tap

Approve

You tap your choice. Links open a short confirm page, so a mail scanner opening links can never act for you.

6On approval

Act

Kangaroo checks that the invite hasn't changed since you approved it, then acts once through Graph and logs it.

Open core

A free core, with paid features

The engine and portal will be open source so anyone can self-host them with their own AI model. The decision engine and the paid plans are Kangaroo's own.

Self-hosted core

Free · open source

Calendar watching, actions, Day in Review and the portal under the AGPL license, running with your own AI model.

Decision engine

Paid

Four clear suggestions, Skip, Catch up later, Ask first or Go, each with short reasons you can check. How it decides stays private.

Hosted plans

Coming soon

Kangaroo runs it for you, with better drafts, richer knowledge sources and US-only AI processing on higher plans.

Enterprise

Coming later

Org-wide policies, single sign-on, analytics and compliance.

The hard part

Problems solved in code

The first three I solved by hand in the account layer. The rest came up in the core engine build.

Several accounts, one user

Auth.js assumes one login per account. I passed the intent (link an identity, calendar or knowledge source) through the OAuth state value, so a reconnect updates the right record instead of creating duplicates.

All-or-nothing signup

The user, their first identity, a 14-day trial and their first calendar are created in one database transaction, so a half-created account can't break later pages.

Revocable sessions

Each session is checked against an active internal user, and changing an ID in a request can't touch another user's records.

Mail scanners clicking buttons

Outlook's link scanner opens every link before the user does. A link never acts on its own: it opens a confirm page, and links are signed and expire.

Working buttons inside Outlook

Taps arrive with a Microsoft-signed token that Kangaroo verifies before acting. The card was rebuilt for the older card format that Mac and phone apps draw.

Never acting twice

Each invite is claimed in one atomic database update, so a double tap does nothing. Kangaroo re-reads the event and stops if it changed or was cancelled.

Subscriptions that expire

Graph calendar subscriptions last about three days. A daily job renews them and re-syncs, so a missed notification is still caught without duplicates.

Each user's own email hour

The free hosting plan only allows daily scheduled jobs, so 24 jobs run one per hour, each sending to users whose chosen hour has come.

Least-privilege mail

Kangaroo reads only the reply thread to its own request for details, and its sending app is limited to a single shared mailbox.

Key decisions

Choices behind the design

Decide inside the invite

Nobody waits for an evening email to decide on invites, so I moved the suggestion and buttons into the invite email itself.

Nothing unfinished reaches users

Every page users can reach is complete, hidden or marked "Coming soon", with a screenshot review on desktop and phone before each release.

Microsoft first

The first customers live in Outlook and OneDrive. Google is planned for later, because Google's Drive read access requires a paid third-party security assessment.

Swappable AI layer

One provider-agnostic module means switching AI models is a settings change, and token usage is logged per invite to check the cost model.

Open source as a clean copy

Development stays private, and the core will be released as a clean copy after the hosted plan is live and the product is proven.

Human approval first

Nothing happens until you tap. Auto-decline will be opt-in, with a daily report and one-click undo, and is marked "Coming soon" until then.

The problem

Prospecting context was scattered everywhere

Salespeople often cover dozens of organizations, many with several sites, while working toward a meeting goal every term. Most of the work to get there is manual: writing every message, piecing together history from many tools, deciding who to contact next and rebuilding the week for reports.

Outreach written by hand

Every email is written one person at a time, which is the biggest time sink.

History in many places

Who you've talked to, about what and when, lives across email, chat, meeting transcripts, the CRM, BI reports and spreadsheets.

No one deciding who's next

It's easy to contact some people too often and forget others, and whole accounts can go untouched for months.

Follow-ups that slipped

Quotes, renewals and stalled deals have no consistent next step, and "check back in the spring" has nothing to bring it back.

Manual Friday reporting

Logging meetings in the CRM and counting the week's calls, messages and emails means rebuilding the week from memory.

Every rep sells differently

Territories, buyers, goals, tools and tone vary from rep to rep, so one fixed set of rules never fits everyone.

One app, built for each rep

The core app is finished and tested, with every view and engine but no one's wording, numbers or territory. A rep attaches it and the setup prompt to a Claude chat and types "Run this." Automation

From copy and paste to hands-off

Copy, paste, review

The app writes each prompt, the rep runs it in their AI assistant and pastes one table back. It works on day one, anywhere, with no IT approval. If the AI can run saved prompts on a schedule, like Copilot's scheduled prompts, the two morning prompts run on their own.

2Start semi, plan for automated

The recommended start

The app works semi-automated now, with the automation built in but switched off. Settings shows it as "Not connected yet," with a two-line note the rep can send IT about what would be connected.

3Automated

Results arrive on their own

A scheduled flow saves each AI result as a file in a synced inbox folder. The app reads it when it opens or when the rep clicks "Check inbox," shows an "Inbox from automation" card on Today for review, and never applies the same file twice. Copy and paste stays as a fallback.

Rules for facts, AI for meaning

Rules decide facts, like whether someone replied, whether a meeting is on the calendar, or whether it's too soon to reach out. The AI decides meaning: what an email is asking, which topic fits and what to say. If a rep's AI can't read their email, the build swaps the check-in for a quick "Who replied?" checklist instead.

How it decides

Persistent without being pushy

Courtesy rules apply on every list, including sequences, the call blitz and suggested contacts. They ship switched off, and the interview always asks for them, starting from three presets.

Who to contact next

A point score ranks everyone eligible: a referral, an upcoming renewal, an existing account or a long quiet spell raise someone, and a recent conversation or a full site lowers them. What's working adjusts the weights.

A real reason every time

Every person needs an approved topic before outreach, found in order: renewal, whitespace, past conversation, public signal, then focus. If nothing real turns up, it says "Needs input."

Opposite-channel re-engage

If the last contact was an email or meeting, the sequence starts with a call. If it was a call, it starts with an email. Someone you know never gets a cold intro.

"Not now" check-backs

The app saves the date and what they said, pauses everything or just that topic, then brings them back on that date with an opener that quotes them.

Held, not lost

A touch that breaks a rule shows as held with the date it comes back, and later steps shift with it instead of piling up. Do-not-contact is honored everywhere.

Stale deals move

Once a deal has been quiet for the number of days the rep chooses, it moves to Hot Follow-ups with a revival draft.

Privacy by design

The rep's data stays on the rep's computer

Each build is one HTML file opened in the browser, so in semi-automated mode there's no new system for IT to approve.

Nothing is sent anywhere

In semi-automated mode the app's code makes no network requests. I confirmed this by searching the source for any network calls.

The AI stays in the rep's account

All searching of email, chat and files happens in the rep's own AI assistant, such as Microsoft 365 Copilot inside an organization's tenant. The app never connects to it.

You see everything that moves

Every prompt is visible before it goes to the AI, and every result is reviewed before it's saved. Outreach is always sent by the rep.

Backup is a file you own

Data lives in browser storage, with a sync file in the rep's own cloud drive folder. It merges item by item and lets the app run on a second PC.

It keeps little

Raw email and calendar content is never stored, only one-line summaries, and the coaching prompt sends result rates, not names.

Built with fictional data

No real customer or company data was used to build or test it, and the setup prompt stops a rep who starts pasting real customer details into the chat.

The hard part

Problems solved

Making one app fit every rep

Instead of a settings page with hundreds of options, Claude edits the code for each rep using a code map of the core app, so a build carries their own wording, rules and views.

Testing a prompt, not just code

Claude sessions played made-up reps from start to finish. An early run that built from a description alone looked too plain, so the prompt now customizes the core app. Later runs found 24 and then 14 gaps in the prompt, which were fixed.

Automation without extra permissions

Fully automatic runs usually need connections and permissions most reps don't have. Scheduled prompts deliver the morning results with no extra access, and the automation routes wait until IT approves.

A local file can't call an AI

Each prompt asks for a table with fixed columns. One paste box recognizes every table by its header row, and a review screen shows each change before saving.

The first approach was too generic

My first Copilot prompts mostly listed roles I was missing, in long chat replies. I made the AI the data gatherer and the app the place where history, rules and judgment come together.

Volume without harassment

Sales managers want volume, and buyers don't want to be chased. Courtesy rules run across every channel and list, plus the "not now" choice of pausing everything or just one topic.

Messy account structure

An organization-to-site model handles single companies, separate site teams, central offices and units that buy for everyone, with name matching for common abbreviations.

Long history, cut-off answers

History comes in two-week chunks, each tagged with a code so one Paste button knows which chunk arrived. "Copy the rest" handles answers the AI cuts off.

Surviving a cleared browser

A sync file in a cloud drive merges item by item, the newest edit wins, and it carries settings and templates too.

Measuring without manual tagging

Every touch records its topic source, focus, channel and step. A reply counts within 21 days, and suggestions wait until there are enough samples.

Keeping AI judgment

Topic suggestions follow a priority order and the rep reviews each one, with no generic topics allowed.

What only the rep knows

Cards ask for things no system has, like close dates, who signs off and next steps, and the answers feed the next AI run.

A few prompts still ask for mailbox access when the AI can't read email, and each app is for one person.
